\section*{Question 1: Real vs.\ Nominal Deficits}

In a given year, Austria's government runs a \textbf{nominal} budget deficit of \texteuro{}10 billion. Inflation that year is 3\%, and the government's outstanding debt at the start of the year is \texteuro{}300 billion.

\qpart{a)} Inflation erodes the real value of existing government debt, which effectively offsets part of the nominal deficit. Calculate the \textbf{real} (inflation-adjusted) budget deficit that year. (Hint: subtract inflation $\times$ outstanding debt from the nominal deficit.)

\qpart{b)} A newspaper headline reads: \emph{``Austria's deficit hits \texteuro{}10 billion --- worse than ever!''} In 2--3 sentences, explain why reporting only the nominal deficit figure can be a misleading way to describe the government's fiscal position, drawing on your answer to part (a) and/or the idea of measuring the deficit relative to GDP.

\section*{Question 2: Tiebout and Local Public Goods}

Two neighboring Austrian municipalities offer very different packages of local public services and property taxes. \emph{Gemeinde X} has excellent local schools and high property taxes; \emph{Gemeinde Y} has minimal local services and low property taxes.

\qpart{a)} Using the Tiebout ``voting with your feet'' logic, explain how allowing people to freely choose where to live can help reveal residents' true preferences for local public goods --- something that is hard to do through voting at the national level.

\qpart{b)} Pick \textbf{one} realistic reason why this Tiebout sorting mechanism works less perfectly in practice than the simple theory suggests (for example: moving costs, imperfect information about what each municipality offers, zoning restrictions, or public-service spillovers that cross municipal borders). Name it and explain in 2--3 sentences why it weakens the sorting mechanism.
