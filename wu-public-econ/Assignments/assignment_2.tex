\section*{Question 1: Discounting and Cost-Benefit Analysis}

The city of Linz is evaluating a new tram line. It costs \texteuro{}40 million today (year 0) and is expected to generate net social benefits of \texteuro{}5 million per year for the next 10 years (years 1--10).

\qpart{a)} Using a discount rate of 5\%, the present value of a \texteuro{}5 million/year benefit stream over 10 years is approximately \texteuro{}38.6 million. Calculate the project's net present value (NPV) at this discount rate. Should the city build the tram line on cost-benefit grounds alone?

\qpart{b)} A city council member argues the discount rate should instead be 2\%, since ``future generations' well-being matters just as much as ours.'' Without recomputing the exact NPV, explain in 2--3 sentences which direction this change would push the project's NPV, and why. Then explain, in one sentence, why the choice of discount rate is often the most contested part of a real-world cost-benefit analysis.

\section*{Question 2: The Median Voter}

Three residents of a small Austrian village must decide, by pairwise majority vote, how large a new public swimming pool should be (measured in square meters). Resident A wants 200~m\textsuperscript{2}, Resident B wants 500~m\textsuperscript{2}, and Resident C wants 900~m\textsuperscript{2}. All three have single-peaked preferences: each resident's satisfaction falls the further the actual size is from their own ideal.

\qpart{a)} According to the median voter theorem, which pool size wins against any other proposal put to a majority vote? Explain briefly why, in terms of what happens when someone proposes a size above or below it.

\qpart{b)} A fourth resident, D, moves into the village. D's preferences are also single-peaked, but D wants a pool of just 50~m\textsuperscript{2} (preferring to spend the money elsewhere). Does the outcome that wins under majority vote change? Explain your reasoning.
