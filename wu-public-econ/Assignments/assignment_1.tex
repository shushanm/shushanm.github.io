\section*{Question 1: Pigouvian Taxation}

A caf\'e on Mariahilferstra\ss e runs outdoor heaters in winter. Let $Q$ be heater-hours per week. The caf\'e's private marginal cost of running the heaters is
\[
PMC(Q) = 2Q,
\]
and each heater-hour imposes a marginal external damage on the neighbors (noise, fumes) of
\[
MD(Q) = Q.
\]
The marginal benefit to caf\'e customers from heater-hours is
\[
MB(Q) = 60 - Q.
\]

\qpart{a)} Write down the social marginal cost $SMC(Q)$. Find the socially efficient quantity of heater-hours, $Q^*$, and the Pigouvian tax per heater-hour that would achieve it.

\qpart{b)} The district council is instead considering a flat rule: every caf\'e on the street may run heaters for at most 15 hours per week, no exceptions. In 2--3 sentences, explain why this uniform quantity cap could be \emph{less} efficient than the Pigouvian tax from part (a), even if it happens to produce the same \emph{total} heater-hours across the street.

\section*{Question 2: Designing a Natural Experiment}

The city of Graz is banning through-traffic in its old town center to cut air pollution. The ban is \textbf{phased in} one neighborhood at a time over 18 months, rather than everywhere at once.

\qpart{a)} A local newspaper wants to claim the ban ``caused'' lower pollution by simply comparing air-quality readings in the old town \emph{before} vs.\ \emph{after} the full ban took effect. In 2--3 sentences, explain why this before/after comparison alone is not credible evidence of a causal effect.

\qpart{b)} The staggered rollout gives you a better option. Briefly describe a difference-in-differences design you could use instead: at any given point during the 18 months, which neighborhoods would be your \emph{treatment} group and which would be your \emph{control} group? What assumption about the control group does this design rely on?
