\section{3.1 Correlation Is Not Causation}

\begin{frame}{The Identification Problem}
  In the early nineteenth century, a cholera epidemic swept through Russia.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The government sent doctors to the worst-affected areas.
    \item Peasants in one province observed a very high \textbf{correlation} between the number of doctors in an area and the incidence of cholera there.
    \item Relying on this fact, they banded together and murdered their doctors.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{alertblock}{What went wrong}
    They correctly saw that doctors and illness moved together --- but the \emph{causal} arrow ran the other way: more illness brought more doctors, not the reverse.
  \end{alertblock}
  \srcnote{Gruber, \emph{Public Finance and Public Policy}, Ch.\ 3.1.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Same Mistake Keeps Happening}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item September 2014: aid workers trying to help communities in Guinea fight an Ebola outbreak were attacked, and eight were killed. A local leader: \emph{``Wherever those people have passed, the communities have been hit by illness.''}
    \item 1988: a Harvard dean found that freshmen who had taken SAT-preparation courses scored, on average, \emph{63 points lower} than those who hadn't --- and concluded the courses were unhelpful.
    \item In fact: in 1998 prep courses were rare, so mostly the \emph{weakest} test-takers --- who needed the most help --- took them. The courses didn't cause worse scores; students likely to score worse were the ones taking the courses.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \centering
  \alert{Statistics calls this the \emph{identification problem}: given that two variables move together, how do you identify what's causing what?}\\
  \srcnote{Gruber, \emph{Public Finance and Public Policy}, Ch.\ 3.1.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three (and Only Three) Explanations for Any Correlation}
  For any correlation between two variables $A$ and $B$:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \(A\) causes \(B\).
    \item \(B\) causes \(A\).
    \item Some third factor causes both.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{alertblock}{Why this matters for policy}
    Policy makers want to use empirical results to \emph{predict} how an intervention will change behavior. Correlation alone provides no predictive power --- prediction requires knowing the \emph{causal} link.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section{3.2 Measuring Causation with Data We'd Like to Have: Randomized Trials}

\begin{frame}{The Gold Standard: Randomized Trials}
  \begin{block}{Randomized trial}
    A group of volunteers is randomly divided into a \textbf{treatment group}, which receives the intervention being studied, and a \textbf{control group}, which does not --- effectively, assignment by the flip of a coin.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Because assignment doesn't depend on anything about the subjects, the two groups are --- on average --- identical in \emph{every} respect except the treatment.
    \item Any remaining difference in outcomes can then be attributed to the treatment: this is what makes randomization the gold standard for estimating causal effects.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Problem of Bias}
  \begin{block}{Bias}
    Any source of difference between treatment and control groups that is correlated with the treatment but is \emph{not due to} the treatment.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Cholera doctors, SAT prep, and breastfeeding studies all suffered from bias: treatment and control groups differed for reasons \emph{other than} the treatment itself.
    \item By definition, a properly randomized trial has \emph{no} such bias --- the only systematic difference between the groups is the treatment.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \centering
  \alert{This is why randomized trials are the benchmark against which every other empirical method is judged.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Real Randomized Trial: Estrogen Replacement Therapy}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Estrogen replacement therapy (ERT) was widely used to treat menopause symptoms. Early \emph{observational} comparisons found ERT users had no higher (perhaps even lower) heart-disease risk.
    \item Concern: ERT users were also more likely to be under a doctor's care, live healthier lifestyles, and have higher incomes --- all independently linked to lower heart-disease risk. This is textbook \emph{bias} (explanation 3: a third factor drives both).
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{alertblock}{The Women's Health Initiative trial}
    A randomized trial of 16,000+ women aged 50--79 across 40 US clinical centers was stopped early (after 5.2 of a planned 8.5 years) because the result was already clear: ERT \emph{raised} heart-disease risk --- 7 more coronary events, 8 more strokes, 8 more pulmonary embolisms, and 8 more invasive breast cancers per 10,000 women per year, relative to control.
  \end{alertblock}
  \srcnote{Gruber, \emph{Public Finance and Public Policy}, Ch.\ 3.2, citing the Women's Health Initiative trial.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Randomized Trials in Public Finance: The Global Rise of RCTs}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Randomized trials are far more common in medicine than in public finance --- but the last two decades have seen a surge, led by the \textbf{MIT Jameel Poverty Action Lab (J-PAL)}, founded 2003 by Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo, and Sendhil Mullainathan.
    \item J-PAL's network has run 1,000+ randomized evaluations of social programs across 91 countries, in partnership with governments, NGOs, and firms.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{alertblock}{Example: Indonesia's Raskin subsidized-rice program}
    Only a third of eligible households received their full rice entitlement, partly because households couldn't prove their eligibility. Researchers randomly gave 378 villages an ID card stating a household's entitlement, versus 194 control villages without one. Card villages saw a sizeable rise in rice actually received --- without more going to ineligible households. Indonesia scaled the card nationwide in 2013.
  \end{alertblock}
  \srcnote{Banerjee, Hanna \& Olken, cited in Gruber, \emph{Public Finance and Public Policy}, Ch.\ 3.2.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A European Randomized Trial: Job-Search Counseling in France}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Public-finance RCTs aren't only a developing-economy story. Behaghel, Crépon \& Gurgand (2014) ran a large controlled experiment across roughly 235 French local labor markets (\(\approx\)44{,}000 job seekers).
    \item Job seekers were randomly assigned to intensive counseling from the \textbf{public} employment service, from \textbf{private} providers, or to a control group receiving standard services.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{alertblock}{Finding}
    Intensive counseling raised job-exit rates by roughly \textbf{15--35\%} relative to control --- and public-service counseling was about twice as effective as private-provider counseling in the first six months.
  \end{alertblock}
  \centering
  A companion study (Crépon, Duflo, Gurgand, Rathelot \& Zamora, 2013) used the same randomization to show job-placement help for some can partly \emph{displace} the job chances of others --- a caution about reading any single RCT's effect as a free lunch.\\
  \srcnote{Behaghel, Crépon \& Gurgand, \emph{AEJ: Applied Economics} 6(4), 2014; Crépon et al., \emph{QJE}, 2013.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why We Need to Go Beyond Randomized Trials}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cost and time}: large trials are expensive and slow to plan and run.
    \item \textbf{Ethics}: a 2001 Parkinson's-disease trial drilled holes in the skulls of all 18 subjects (to keep the control group comparable) but implanted fetal pig cells in only 10 --- widely criticized.
    \item \textbf{External validity}: results are only valid for people who \emph{volunteer} to be treatments or controls, who may differ systematically from the population at large.
    \item \textbf{Attrition}: if people leave the trial non-randomly (e.g., those with negative side effects drop out), the remaining sample is no longer representative.
  \end{itemize}
  \srcnote{Gruber, \emph{Public Finance and Public Policy}, Ch.\ 3.2.}
\end{frame}

\section{3.3 Estimating Causation with the Data We Actually Get}

\begin{frame}{Observational Data}
  \begin{block}{Observational data}
    Data generated by individual behavior observed in the real world, not in the context of a deliberately designed experiment.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  Most of the time, this is all we have. The rest of this lecture covers three approaches researchers use to get \emph{as close as possible} to the gold standard using observational data:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Time series analysis
    \item Cross-sectional regression analysis
    \item Quasi-experiments (incl.\ difference-in-differences)
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Time Series Analysis}
  \begin{block}{Time series analysis}
    Documenting the correlation between two variables of interest \emph{over time}.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Simplest possible empirical approach: plot both series across years and see whether they move together.
    \item \textbf{Problem}: when both series follow a slow-moving trend, almost \emph{anything} else that trends over the same period will look correlated with it --- the correlation may reflect a shared trend, not causation.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

  \begin{center}

  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When Is Time Series Analysis Useful?}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Slow, gradual trends are \emph{not} convincing --- too many other things trend at the same time.
    \item \textbf{Sharp, temporary breaks} are more convincing: if $X$ reverses direction abruptly and $Y$ reverses \emph{at the same moment}, it's harder to find a third factor that just happens to reverse on the identical date.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{alertblock}{A sharp break: Ireland's 2002 plastic-bag levy}
    In March 2002, Ireland introduced a \texteuro{}0.15 levy per plastic shopping bag --- one precisely dated policy change. Convery, McDonnell \& Ferreira (2007) document a reduction in plastic-bag use ``in the order of 90\%'' at retail outlets; a widely-cited companion figure (Irish government) puts per-capita bag consumption at 328/year before the levy versus 21/year after (\(\approx\)95\%).
  \end{alertblock}
  \centering
  The drop happens \emph{exactly} at the levy's start date, not gradually --- hard to explain with a coincidental third factor. That is what makes a sharp break more convincing than a slow trend.\\
  \srcnote{Convery, McDonnell \& Ferreira, ``The Most Popular Tax in Europe? Lessons from the Irish Plastic Bags Levy,'' \emph{Environmental and Resource Economics} 38(1), 2007.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cross-Sectional Regression Analysis}
  \begin{block}{Cross-sectional regression analysis}
    A statistical method for assessing the relationship between two (or more) variables \emph{while holding other factors constant}, comparing many individuals at \emph{one point in time}.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item In its simplest form (\emph{bivariate regression}), it formalizes correlation: it finds the line that \emph{best fits} a scatter of data points, and reports its slope.
    \item The \textbf{dependent variable} is what you want to explain; the \textbf{independent variable(s)} are what you think explain it.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Illustrative Example (Numbers Made Up for Teaching)}
  \footnotesize
  Suppose two \emph{Mindestsicherung} (social-assistance) recipients, Anna and Beatrix, have different preferences for leisure versus work.
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \begin{axis}[
      width=9.5cm, height=4.6cm,
      xmin=0, xmax=1300,
      ymin=0, ymax=1100,
      xlabel={Mindestsicherung (\texteuro{}/month)},
      ylabel={hours worked per year},
      axis lines*=left,
      ]
      \addplot[wublue, thick, mark=*, mark size=2.2pt] coordinates {(700,900) (1000,90)};
      \node[font=\scriptsize] at (axis cs:700,830) {Anna};
      \node[font=\scriptsize] at (axis cs:1000,150) {Beatrix};
    \end{axis}
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  Anna, with a low preference for leisure, works more \emph{and} --- because benefits phase out with earnings --- ends up with lower Mindestsicherung. A bivariate regression of hours on benefits would show a strong \emph{negative} slope.
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \centering
  \alert{But is this slope causal?}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Problems with Cross-Sectional Regression: Bias Again}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item In the Anna/Beatrix example, we know \emph{by construction} that the true story runs the other way: a high preference for leisure causes both \emph{lower} hours worked \emph{and} \emph{higher} Mindestsicherung (since benefits rise as earned income falls).
    \item The negative correlation is \textbf{not causal} --- it reflects a difference in preferences that happens to be correlated with benefit levels.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \centering
  With real data we don't observe ``preference for leisure'' directly --- but the same bias risk remains: people with a low taste for work may simply be both lower earners \emph{and} higher benefit recipients, with no causal link running from benefits to hours at all.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Control Variables}
  \begin{block}{Control variables}
    Variables included in a regression to account for differences between comparison groups that could otherwise bias the estimate.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If we had a perfect measure of ``preference for leisure,'' we could compare only people with the \emph{same} preference --- removing that source of bias entirely.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{alertblock}{The catch}
    The variables we really want (intrinsic preferences, motivation, ability) are usually impossible to measure. We approximate them with proxies --- age, education, past employment --- which only partially capture the underlying difference. Control variables reduce bias; they rarely eliminate it.
  \end{alertblock}
  \srcnote{Gruber, \emph{Public Finance and Public Policy}, Ch.\ 3.3.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Quasi-Experiments}
  \begin{block}{Quasi-experiment}
    A situation that arises naturally when a change in the economic environment (typically, a policy change) creates treatment and control groups that are \emph{nearly identical} except for that change --- letting an outside event do the randomizing for us.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \centering
  Austria's federal structure --- nine \emph{Länder}, each able to set some of its own policy parameters (Lecture 1) --- is a natural source of quasi-experiments: a reform in one Land, with neighbors unaffected, creates a ready-made treatment/control comparison.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Illustrative Example (Numbers Made Up for Teaching)}
  \footnotesize
  Suppose two neighboring Bundesländer, \emph{Vorarlberg} and \emph{Tirol}, both have single-parent Mindestsicherung recipients. In 2023, Vorarlberg cuts its monthly benefit; Tirol's stays unchanged.
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
    \toprule
     & \textbf{2022} & \textbf{2024} & \textbf{Difference} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Vorarlberg} (benefit) & \texteuro{}1{,}000 & \texteuro{}800 & $-$\texteuro{}200 \\
    Vorarlberg (hours/year) & 1{,}000 & 1{,}200 & $+$200 \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Tirol} (benefit) & \texteuro{}1{,}000 & \texteuro{}1{,}000 & \texteuro{}0 \\
    Tirol (hours/year) & 1{,}050 & 1{,}100 & $+$50 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Vorarlberg's raw before/after change (+200 hours) mixes the \emph{benefit cut} with anything else moving nationally (e.g., an economic upswing) --- exactly the time series problem. Tirol didn't experience the cut, but \emph{did} experience the same national trend.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Difference-in-Differences}
  \begin{block}{Difference-in-difference estimator}
    The difference between the change in outcomes for the treatment group and the change in outcomes for the control group.
  \end{block}
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \text{hours(Vorarlberg,24)} - \text{hours(Vorarlberg,22)} &= \text{treatment effect} + \text{national-trend bias} \\
      \text{hours(Tirol,24)} - \text{hours(Tirol,22)} &= \text{national-trend bias} \\
      \hline
      \text{difference} &= \text{treatment effect}
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  In the made-up numbers above: \((+200) - (+50) = \mathbf{150}\) additional hours, \emph{net of} the national trend --- our diff-in-diff estimate of the causal effect of the benefit cut.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Problems with Quasi-Experimental Analysis}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Diff-in-diff assumes the \textbf{parallel-trends} assumption: absent the reform, treatment and control would have moved \emph{the same way}.
    \item If the national trend hit Vorarlberg and Tirol \emph{differently} (e.g., their labor markets are exposed to different industries), the ``bias'' terms are not equal --- and simple subtraction no longer isolates the true treatment effect.
    \item Quasi-experiments can never be \emph{certain} they've purged all bias, the way a true randomized trial can. Researchers argue their case both intuitively (the groups look comparable) and statistically (checking the result against alternative control groups).
  \end{itemize}
  \srcnote{Gruber, \emph{Public Finance and Public Policy}, Ch.\ 3.3.}
\end{frame}

\section{Structural Modeling}

\begin{frame}{Reduced-Form vs.\ Structural Estimates}
  \begin{block}{Reduced-form estimates}
    Measures of the total impact of one variable on another, without decomposing \emph{why} --- what randomized trials and quasi-experiments naturally deliver.
  \end{block}
  \begin{block}{Structural estimates}
    Estimates of the deeper features (e.g., the utility-function parameters from Lecture 2) that drive the response --- letting us decompose an effect into, say, income vs.\ substitution effects.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \centering
  Structural models can tell us \emph{why}; but they require assuming a specific form for preferences, and are wrong if that assumption is wrong. This course, like most of the empirical literature, mostly relies on reduced-form evidence.
  \srcnote{Gruber, \emph{Public Finance and Public Policy}, Ch.\ 3.3.}
\end{frame}

\section{3.4 Conclusion}

\begin{frame}{Highlights}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Correlation is not causation: any correlation between $A$ and $B$ could mean $A \to B$, $B \to A$, or a third factor driving both.
    \item Randomized trials remove bias by construction and are the gold standard --- but are costly, sometimes unethical, and imperfectly generalizable.
    \item Time series analysis is convincing only around sharp, dated breaks --- not slow trends.
    \item Cross-sectional regression can reduce, but rarely eliminates, bias --- control variables are only as good as the proxies available.
    \item Quasi-experiments exploit real-world policy changes to approximate randomization; difference-in-differences is the workhorse tool, resting on a parallel-trends assumption.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Discussion Questions for Next Time}
  \small
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item A researcher finds that Austrian Gemeinden with newly built schools have higher average test scores. Does this prove new schools raise test scores? What else could explain it?
    \item Why might a diff-in-difference comparison of two Austrian Länder be more convincing than comparing a single Land's outcomes before and after a reform?
    \item Under what conditions would you trust an observational (non-randomized) estimate of a policy's effect nearly as much as a randomized trial?
    \item Is it more ethical, or less, to run a randomized trial of a welfare-benefit cut on real recipients than to infer its effect from indirect (observational) evidence alone?
    \item Structural models require assuming a specific utility function; reduced-form models don't. What do we gain, and what do we risk, by making that assumption?
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
